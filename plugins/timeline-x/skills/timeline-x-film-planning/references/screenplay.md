# Screenplay Development Reference

Read this reference when the requested deliverable is a logline, treatment,
beat sheet, screenplay, dialogue revision, character/world bible, or the story
portion of a short-film production plan.

## Story contract

Define these before drafting long-form pages:

- **Protagonist:** the person whose consequential choices organize the film.
- **Want:** the concrete result they actively pursue.
- **Need or pressure:** the internal truth, fear, contradiction, or value being
  tested; not every story requires a therapeutic transformation.
- **Obstacle:** an active opposing force, circumstance, relationship, or flaw.
- **Stakes:** what becomes harder, is lost, or is exposed if they fail.
- **Theme:** the question or tension dramatized by choices, not a slogan stated
  by dialogue.
- **Genre promise:** the kind of tension, pleasure, pacing, and resolution the
  audience should experience.
- **Ending:** the decisive action, emotional turn, and final image. Work
  backward to plant what that ending needs.

A useful logline usually contains protagonist, inciting situation or goal,
opposition, and stakes in one or two sentences. Do not overload it with cast
lists, lore, or camera language.

## Find the angle

Whatever comes to mind first is what everyone else thinks of too. Before
settling on a premise, draft ten one-line takes on the user's idea for yourself,
changing whose story it is, its scale, how much time it spans, its genre, and
who tells it. Discard the earliest three. Of the rest, keep the three that are
both unexpected and honest: someone hearing the premise would not guess them,
yet they grow from the idea and its world without forcing either, and in
hindsight they feel inevitable. Choose one and tell the user in a single
sentence why it beats the others; if the user has not settled on a reading,
offer the remaining two as alternatives. Do not show the full list of ten.

Two independent checks sharpen the choice:

- **Prediction:** when the host can start a fresh helper, give it only the
  user's request and ask for the five pieces anyone would expect from it.
  Replace a kept angle that shares a subject, key image, or form with one of
  them.
- **Repetition:** when the user's script library is available, search it with
  `list_film_scripts` and read a close match with `get_film_script`. Drop an
  angle that repeats a saved story's premise and key images unless the user
  asks for a sequel or the same subject again. A brand, product, person, or
  place the user named is theirs and never counts as repetition on its own.

## One turn

A short piece has room for a single, legible change. State it before writing
beats, as "Before: … / After: …". Kinds worth reaching for:

- **Reversal:** two roles trade places, for example the guard becomes the
  guarded or the small thing outgrows the large one.
- **Reveal:** the frame opens out from a fragment, and the full picture changes
  what the fragment meant.
- **Transformation:** one place or object seen at different times, its final
  state replying to its first.
- **Threshold:** just before something happens, the happening, and its result.
- **Mirror:** the closing image echoes the opening one, now read differently.
- **Contrast:** narration and image disagree, and the disagreement carries the
  meaning.

A portrait or mood piece can do without a change. In that case state the
feeling or thought it should leave behind, and keep fresh material arriving so
it never stalls.

## Structure follows the selected form

- **Three-act:** establish the unstable normal, force a commitment, escalate
  attempts and consequences, create a reversal or lowest point, then resolve
  through a decisive choice.
- **Single scene:** enter after the situation has begun, give each character an
  immediate objective, escalate tactics and revelations inside one place/time,
  turn the power relationship, and leave soon after the decisive change.
- **Montage:** establish a visual rule, vary and escalate it, use contrast and
  compression to show progression, break the pattern at the dramatic turn, and
  land on a resolving image. Montage is not a list of unrelated attractive
  shots.
- **Nonlinear:** give the audience an anchor question, make every time shift
  purposeful and legible, repeat motifs with changed meaning, and converge or
  recontextualize events. Confusion is not automatically mystery.

These are diagnostic patterns, not mandatory formulas. Preserve a different
structure explicitly requested by the user.

## Beat and scene design

Each beat should have a causal relationship to what follows: “because of this”
or “but then,” not only “and then.” For every scene, identify:

1. whose scene it is and what they want now;
2. what resists them;
3. what tactic or choice changes the encounter;
4. the turn—new information, power shift, emotional reversal, or irreversible
   action;
5. the consequence that launches or alters the next scene.

Enter late, once pressure is active, and exit after the meaningful change.
Remove scenes that only repeat information, tone, or outcome. Combine scenes
when one stronger conflict can do both jobs.

## Screenplay page conventions

Use a clean, readable screenplay form unless the user requests another format:

```text
INT./EXT. LOCATION – TIME

Present-tense visual action in short paragraphs. Describe what can be seen or
heard, not unfilmable explanation.

                         CHARACTER
                 Dialogue that can be spoken.
```

- Start a new heading when interior/exterior, location, or meaningful time
  changes.
- Introduce an important character in uppercase once, with only playable
  identity details relevant to the film.
- Keep action paragraphs focused and visual. Avoid prose-novel interior
  monologue unless it is expressed through behavior, image, or voiceover.
- Use parentheticals sparingly and only when the line's intention would
  otherwise be unclear.
- Use transitions and camera directions only when essential to the storytelling
  or when writing an explicitly requested shooting script.
- A screenplay page is often roughly a minute, but dialogue density, silence,
  action, and format can shift timing. Read dialogue aloud and estimate visual
  actions rather than padding to a page count.

## Dialogue and voiceover

Give each speaker a distinct vocabulary, rhythm, directness, and strategy.
Characters often speak to achieve something, conceal something, or test the
other person. Prefer subtext over explaining emotion already visible in action.

Voiceover should add perspective, contradiction, compression, or information
the image cannot carry. It should not narrate every visible action. Silent
stories still need precise behavioral beats and sound design opportunities.

Keep lines speakable within the intended duration. When timing is tight, reduce
redundancy before changing delivery speed or cutting meaning.

Pick the narration register for each story instead of reusing a default. The
range runs from no voice at all through a warm documentary tone, a playful or
deadpan one (an even delivery against absurd images), a close first-person
confession, a teller of tales, a child's voice, an artificial voice, or
several voices together. Settle who is speaking, when the voice first enters,
and whom it addresses, then hold that register to the end.
Time lines from a measured temp read, either the user's own recording or a read
made within the approved voiceover scope; real line lengths drive shot timing.

## Real people and events

When the subject is real, every statement needs a source you can point to.
Keep a claims table with one row per date, figure, quotation, or deed: the
statement, where it comes from, and whether that source confirms it. Drop an
unconfirmed statement or mark it as reported ("anlatılana göre", "iddiaya
göre"), and never round a figure in the direction that makes it stronger.
Separate what happened from what the piece makes of it: commentary is allowed,
but it must not be phrased as fact. If a source cannot be reached, note that
and leave the gap open; never fill it from memory.

## Continuity bible

Record only facts production must preserve:

- character face/body traits, age range, hair, wardrobe, accessories, and
  emotional/physical state;
- recurring props, which hand holds them, damage/state changes, and ownership;
- location layout, entrances, eyelines, screen direction, and time of day;
- weather, light direction/quality, palette, texture, and recurring motif;
- dialogue and action overlaps, off-screen sound, music state, and intentional
  silence.

Distinguish fixed facts from scene-to-scene changes. A continuity change is
valid when the story causes it and the plan records when it happens.

## Production handoff

Do not convert one screenplay scene into one generated clip automatically.
First preserve the dramatic scene, then design the fewest shots needed for
clarity, performance, emphasis, and editability. A production shot should state
one primary visible action and one camera idea. Put dialogue responsibility on
the correct shot or mark it as off-screen; plan reaction and listening coverage
when the cut needs it.

For generated shots, use reference or inherited frames only when continuity
needs them. Independent shots should remain independent so the queue can run in
parallel. Never let a provider duration limit rewrite story causality, character
identity, the chosen ending, or dialogue mode without user approval.

## Final revision pass

Check, in order:

1. premise, desire, obstacle, stakes, and ending agree;
2. causal logic and character decisions are understandable;
3. every scene has conflict, a turn, and a consequence;
4. setup/payoff, geography, props, time, wardrobe, and knowledge are continuous;
5. dialogue is distinct, speakable, and non-redundant;
6. tone and genre promise remain consistent while allowing contrast;
7. estimated runtime matches the requested duration;
8. for a real subject, each statement appears in the claims table with its source;
9. the delivered format is exactly what the user asked for—not an unsolicited
   shot list, Scene Manager plan, generation prompt set, or finished film.
