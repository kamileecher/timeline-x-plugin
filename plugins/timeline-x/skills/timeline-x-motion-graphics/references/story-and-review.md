# Story and review: the one thing, the hook, beats, copy, and the review pass

Read the story half before planning any Motion Graphics piece that tells
something over about 15 s: a launch, an explainer, a changelog, a recap, a
narrative music piece. Most weak pieces are good motion around no story: a
feature list, three cards in a row, a logo, an address. Skip it for an edit to
an existing project, captions or overlays on footage, and a short single-idea
graphic, where the request is the story. When the user gave a script, a
storyboard or a reference video, follow theirs; when a workflow has its own
concept gate (app promo, film planning), that gate runs and these rules feed
it. Read the review half before presenting any new piece, and after any edit
that changes timing or layout. The shape of each kind of piece is in
[video types](video-types.md).

## The one thing

- What it is, in one line: what the viewer can now do (a launch), the one idea
  they must understand (an explainer), what changed and why it matters (a
  changelog). A whole release is not one thing: take its headline.
- What the viewer should remember afterwards, in their words, not the slogan.
- One viewer doing one real task, from the moment they need it to the result.
  That task is the spine, and one real piece of data (a query, a file, a
  number) travels through it from the hook to the payoff. An explainer's spine
  is one worked example.
- Keep facts (what it does, real numbers, real labels) apart from creative
  choices (angle, tone, look). Every claim on screen traces to the source: the
  product, the docs, the brief or the user's words. Use the user's own copy
  verbatim.
- What the source does not say stays unsaid. Do not turn two measured times
  into a multiplier the source never stated, mark an example figure as
  illustrative, and show a screen you cannot get as a labelled placeholder,
  never a drawn imitation.

## Three storylines, then one

Pitch three different pieces, not three titles on one piece. Each has its own
world (where it happens and how it looks), its own device (what the story is)
and its own form (how the camera and cuts carry it). Sketch five or more
one-line pairings of a device and a form first, drop those the subject cannot
carry, and keep the three that differ most; make one of them the bold one you
would not have picked first. Each storyline states:

- its name, device and form, and in two sentences what the viewer sees and
  feels;
- exactly what is on screen in the first two seconds;
- its beats on one line (hook → … → end card);
- its look on one line: ground, type, and the one material or treatment, taken
  from the subject or the brand;
- its sound on one line: the bed's feel and tempo, the one moment the music
  changes, which actions get effects, or silence and why.

Storylines are words: build nothing before one is picked. Recommend the one a
stranger would follow and remember, with people or a situation they know,
ahead of the most striking picture, and give the reason in one clause. When the
user is there to answer, show the three and stop until they pick; a mix of two
is one storyline. Put everything else in one assumption line: length as a
range, aspect, music or voice, and every paid item with its free alternative.
When the user asked for no stops, build your recommendation and name the other
two in one line each. Keep the unbuilt two in the plan so a later turn can
switch.

**Devices:**

| Device | What the viewer sees |
| --- | --- |
| A moment in someone's day | a person, a time, the message that creates the need |
| A character does the work | a mascot or pointer carries, drags and reacts; every cut has a cause |
| The feature as the hook | the feature acting on something recognisable in the first half second |
| Result first | the finished output plays, then the camera pulls back to where it came from |
| One object across apps | the thing being made travels from another tool into the product |
| Before and after | the old way as a scene, then the new way; the new side calm while the old churns |
| Type carries the argument | large kinetic type and colour fields cutting on the beat |
| A number lands | one real number earned by a scene, then made physical |
| A question holding the input | "What if you could …" with the blank filled by a real input |
| The object is the explanation | a machine or model whose working is the idea, toured by the camera |

**Forms:**

| Form | How it is built here |
| --- | --- |
| One continuous take | one asset or a Series with a single camera progress that pushes from one world into the next |
| Object-to-object chain | every shot ends on the thing that opens the next; morph or a Timeline match carries it |
| Endless zoom | a push into a detail that becomes the next world, repeated |
| A journey through one world | a map (route-map), a desk or a 3D Studio set; each idea is a stop |
| Cut on the beat | hard cuts on bar markers, type and colour fields changing every half beat |
| Match cuts | one shape holds while everything around it changes |
| Split screen to one | several panes play, then collapse into the one that matters |
| One frame that rebuilds | a single composition keeps rearranging and never leaves the frame |

A pitch where all three are plain cuts between scenes has not tried the forms.
Never mock a named competitor. A recap asks one question first so each piece
answers it.

## The hook

The first two seconds are the densest of the piece, and frame 0 is the
thumbnail: never black, never a logo alone. Open on the feature working on
something recognisable, a real moment that creates the need, a question with
the input inside it, a sentence whose last word becomes the picture, or the
problem itself. Then let the old way fail on screen (the wait, the wall of
text, the wrong result) before the product brings relief; the proof is often
the same shot improved in front of the viewer. Typing into a prompt box as the
opener, with nothing else on screen, is a tell; a few seconds in, as a story
step, it is fine.

## Beats

A common shape for a launch of about 35 s; scale it, let the device bend it,
and take another type's shape from [video types](video-types.md):

| Beat | Share | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook | about 8% | the world and the need, or the feature already working |
| Tension | about 12% | the old way failing on screen, or a gag |
| Reveal | about 8% | the product or idea named as a title, 20–30% into the piece |
| Proof | about 40% | the real task in the real interface as three to six verbs, one beat each |
| Payoff | about 20% | the output arrives already moving: the biggest moment, once, 60–80% in |
| Claim | about 8% | the one thing, in one line |
| End card | about 8% | the mark carried in, the name and the call to action land and hold |

In each verb beat keep only what the action touches and what proves the line.
The process runs fast; the payoff plays at normal speed. Length follows the
story (a feature launch is usually 20–45 s); a length in the request is a
range, never padded.

## Worlds

A piece visits several worlds, each with its own ground, light and scale (a
desktop, the product on a brand field, a white void with a character, live
footage), never one dark canvas with small interface floating in it. Enter each
world through an object already on screen: the thing clicked becomes the next
shot. Keep one home stage to return to and one carrier the eye follows, and
hold palette and type constant across worlds.

## Copy

- Titles: at most six words a line, one line on screen at a time, no trailing
  period; an explainer's lines may run to about ten. Hold a line about 0.5 s
  plus 0.3 s per word, and the last line at least 2 s. Shorter identity labels
  follow the app promo timing.
- Show, do not list: no bullet slides or three-card rows. When similar things
  repeat, each gets its own idea and layout.
- Real numbers only, from the source. The first line is never "Introducing",
  "Meet" or the logo; after the problem, an introduction is fine. Avoid stock
  claims such as unlock, seamless, supercharge, game-changer, revolutionary,
  effortless or "in seconds" (in Turkish: zahmetsiz, sorunsuz, devrim
  niteliğinde, saniyeler içinde) unless measured; `check_script_text` with kind
  on_screen catches many of them.
- Read the lines alone: a stranger gets the pitch. Watch the pictures alone:
  they tell the same story.
- No narration unless the request or the source calls for one; then the screen
  never repeats the spoken sentence word for word. Without a voice, large
  titles and the product's own text carry the words over music and effects.

## The brand

The mark lands once, with weight, usually on the end card; before that the
brand shows only inside the product and the story, never docked small in a
corner. The end card is built, not a still: the mark is carried in, typed or
wiped in. Ration the accent colour: one highlighted word, the payoff, the end
card.

## Tells of a generated piece

The defaults a piece falls into when nothing is decided: a dark void with small
things floating in it; glow and particles; the cream paper, serif and small
monospace label look; everything fading and sliding up; three cards in a row;
the docked logo. Beyond those, for a story:

- every scene entering the same way, or the same move beat after beat;
- side-by-side panels and floating cards standing in for a scene;
- titles that keep sliding or shrinking after they land;
- breathing or floating loops standing in for motion on titles and interface
  (a character's idle breath is acting, not a tell).

When a draft shows any of them, name it, take the replacement from the subject,
the brand or the medium, and add it to a banned line in the plan so later
scenes avoid it too. The per-type tells are at the end of
[video types](video-types.md).

## The review pass

You wrote the timing, so you know what happens; the viewer sees it once, at
full speed, often on a phone. Review as a demanding motion director, not a
proud author. Tool checks find what is broken; this pass finds what is weak.
It complements the motion review in [motion craft](motion-craft.md) and the
verification steps in the main skill, and it runs before the visual analysis
review rounds a finished draft goes through with the user.

### 1. Measure, then look

1. Run `review_motion_graphic` on every new or changed asset and read its
   findings: text_too_brief (a line gone before it can be read), motion_frozen
   (a still stretch), no_stagger, content_overlap and low_contrast. A finding is
   a question, not a rule: fix it, or keep it on purpose and say why in the
   delivery note.
2. Over the placed piece, run `audit_timeline_quality` for caption timing,
   title-safe placement and gaps, and `analyze_text_contrast` on the busiest
   frames. Check that frame 0 is not empty with `capture_canvas_frame` at time
   zero, that no stretch over about 3 s goes without a visible change, that
   readable text stays above about 2.6 percent of the short side, and that a
   piece over 15 s that tells something has sound.
3. Look at three sheets: `inspect_visible_shots` for each shot just after it
   starts, a `capture_canvas_frame` at the middle of each beat, and a
   `capture_canvas_strip` across every cut from about 0.2 s before to 0.2 s
   after. Then look at the beat frames at phone size: `capture_canvas_frame`
   with max_edge 640 for 9:16 (about 360 px wide) or 360 for 16:9.
4. For motion between beats, take `capture_canvas_strip` around the hero move
   and the payoff; use `inspect_timeline_frames` when every frame matters.
5. For sound, measure with `analyze_mix_loudness` per section and, after
   export, `audit_export_content`. You cannot hear it: say so.

Where the numbers and your eye disagree, trust the numbers: a clipped label or
a 0.6 s line looks fine on a contact sheet.

### 2. What to look for

First describe each beat frame in one sentence as a stranger would, naming
every object. An object you would have to explain ("those bars are mirrors")
is a P0. Then read the beat frames in order: does a stranger follow the story,
each frame causing the next? Compare each with its planned beat; a mismatch is
a note. Name every tell the frames show and fix those before anything else.
Then go through the sheets once per question:

| Question | What it catches |
| --- | --- |
| Does it read as what it is? | a shape that reads as another object, a material that reads as plastic, a product screen that reads as a mockup, a face that reads wrong |
| Does the subject separate? | subject and ground too close in value, two focal points, nowhere for the eye to go |
| Framing and contact | crops, text at the edge or under platform controls, collisions at a seam, things floating that should touch |
| Timing for a first-time viewer | a read shorter than its words, something popping on with no move, a jump at a cut, everything moving at once on one curve |
| Tells | which general, per-type and motion tells are on screen |
| The pictures alone | with the words covered, do the beat frames still tell the story? |
| The phone sheet | can every line meant to be read be read at phone size? |

### 3. Notes and severity

Write each problem as one line: time · where · problem → exact change · what
stays. For example: "4.2 s · price card · line gone after 0.6 s → hold to
1.9 s · position and entrance unchanged". Rank them:

- **P0** breaks the read or the contract: unreadable, wrong, broken, invented,
  or a tell in the hero shot. Never deliver with a P0 open.
- **P1** weakens the piece: a dead stretch, a soft payoff, a seam that jumps.
- **P2** polish.

Fix every P0 and the P1s worth their time, in this order: text, then timing,
then detail. Take a `checkpoint` before the fixes so a change that makes things
worse can be undone. Repair in place through the main skill, then re-check only
what changed, once; run a second full pass only while a P0 is still open. When
a fix does not hold, capture the frames on either side of the problem, list
every difference, and change only what causes it. A piece that feels busy:
remove about a third of the motion and keep the moves that lead the eye. For an
outside opinion, the fresh reviewer in the visual analysis review rounds takes
only the frames and the spoken lines, never the plan.

### 4. The delivery note

Put a short note in the reply, and when the work continues in another
conversation keep it with the project's context note:

- the creative calls you made, and the storylines you did not build;
- what you measured and what you only looked at; what nobody checked (sound is
  measured, never heard);
- the weakest shot and what you would change with more time;
- findings you kept on purpose, and why.

The next turn reads it before changing anything.
