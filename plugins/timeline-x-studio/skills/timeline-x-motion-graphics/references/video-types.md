# Video types: what each kind of piece needs

Read this after the one thing and the storyline are settled
([story and review](story-and-review.md)) and you know which kind of piece
you are making. Each section gives the shape, the rules that were measured on
finished pieces, and the tells that mark a draft as generated. Authoring,
placement and verification stay with the main skill; a software promo also
follows the app promo workflow, whose gates win where both speak.

## Explainers, tutorials and "how it works"

One idea the viewer understands by the end. The spine is one concrete example
worked from start to finish, not a tour of facts. Usually 30–90 s.

**Start where the viewer is.** Open on a person, a place or a situation the
viewer knows and the question they would ask about it. The abstract device (a
diagram, a model, a graph) enters inside that situation as the answer, and the
piece returns to the person at the end with the answer in hand. A piece that
opens on the device alone is a diagram, not an explanation.

**Devices that explain:**

- The object is the explanation: build the thing whose working is the idea (a
  machine, a model, a map), drawn so it reads as what it is, and take every
  number on screen from it. Tour it with cutaways, an exploded view or a push
  into the part that matters (the parallax camera in motion craft).
- One diagram that grows: start from the simplest true picture and add one
  part per beat, each drawn on (drawPath) when it is named. Never drop a
  finished diagram on screen at once.
- Continuous transformation: each step grows out of the last (a bar becomes an
  axis, a dot becomes a crowd) with morph or a Timeline match, so the viewer
  never loses the thread to a cut.
- The worked example: one real input (a sentence, a number, a photo) travels
  through every step to the answer.

**Pick the shape before the picture.** Most explainable lines are one of a few
shapes: a number that lands, a myth turned over (the wrong idea struck through,
the right one written over it), before and after, many things gathering into a
few groups, a network that fills in, one chosen out of many, steps in order, a
real screenshot with the detail circled, a quote set as a poster, a chapter
card. Choose the shape first, then the picture, so similar lines get different
pictures.

**Words:**

- Narrated: the voice is the clock. Reveal each part when the voice names it,
  spread across the back half of the scene, and keep a scene to about 19
  spoken words (the voiceover workflow's narration reference has the pacing).
- Not narrated: large lines carry it, up to about 10 words each, held 0.5 s
  plus 0.3 s per word.
- Labels sit on the part they name, with a leader line when needed (the
  leader-label Motif), and arrive with it. No legends, no bullet slides.

**Tutorials on real software.** When a pointer is part of the lesson, make it
large (about 4–7 percent of the frame height), bring it in from off-screen, and
let its click cause the next change on the same frame. Push in on the control
being used at 1.6–2 times its real size (the talking-head finishing reference
covers capture resolution), then pull back so the viewer knows where it lives.

**Getting it right.** Every claim, value and step traces to the source; a
number the source did not give is left out or marked illustrative. Keep the
figures in one shared data constant (a Motion Graphic module when several
assets use them) so they agree everywhere. Check the logic of each step before
animating it: a beautiful wrong diagram is worse than none. End on the one idea
in one line; no brand end card unless asked.

**Tells:** a slide of bullets; icons in circles; three cards in a row; the
whole diagram at once; narration repeating the on-screen text word for word.

## Numbers, charts and data stories

**One number at a time.** A number is earned by the scene before it, then made
physical: a count that becomes a skyline, a share that becomes a crowd (the
dot-grid Motif is one mark per unit). The number is the largest thing on
screen, in tabular figures (type craft). A count-up runs 1.2–1.6 s, then
holds, landing on a downbeat or a hard stop. Real data only, rounded the same
way everywhere, with a small source line on screen.

**Charts as pictures:**

- Build in reading order: frame and axis, a reference line, the data drawing
  in, then one annotation on the point that matters. Annotate on the mark.
- Highlight one series and grey the rest. A chart with one thing to say says
  it in its title.
- Carry the same object across beats: a bar becomes the ground of the next
  scene, a line becomes the road the camera follows. The camera may dive into
  the one value that matters while the rest falls away.

**Tells:** a grid of KPI cards; a dashboard; a pie chart; every number counting
up at once; a number with no source.

## Product launch and promo

The app promo workflow owns the brief, the storyboard gate and the shot list.
These rules add to it.

**Real interface, never a mockup.** Everything that claims to be the product is
the product; one invented button loses every viewer who uses it. Sources, best
first: the user's recordings and screenshots (ask per beat, naming the screen,
when the proof needs one you cannot get); walking the flow through a selected
browser connection when no sign-in is needed; the product's own pages and docs
to confirm today's labels. A missing screen becomes a clearly labelled
placeholder ("Screenshot: Settings → Export"), and the reply says so. A feature
with nothing visible (speed, an API) is shown through the real interface that
exposes it, a real analogue the viewer knows, real command output, or a list of
real facts; never a made-up dashboard. Logos, icons and fonts are the brand's
own files.

**The product performs.** Captured screens establish what is real; they are
not the shot list.

1. Establish the workspace: enough real navigation and surroundings to locate
   the task.
2. Isolate the action: bring the field, chart or result forward at about
   1.6–2 times its real size with its real labels and values, and keep the type
   above the type-craft floor after the push. Let the trace draw, the result
   fill or the panel open before moving on. A camera move over a frozen
   screenshot is not the product doing anything.
3. Return the result: carry the same component back into its place, or pull
   back to show where the output is used. A film made only of floating
   close-ups loses the system they belong to, and a rearranged component must
   not imply an interaction the product lacks.

Let containers grow into each other (a button becomes the menu, a message
becomes the bubble) with morph or a Timeline match instead of cutting. When the
ground or palette changes, change every scene that shows it, recheck text and
chart contrast on the new ground, and keep the product's status colours.

**Brand colour.** The accent is the colour that recurs across the most brand
roles. Default link blue and status reds and greens are never the accent. Use
only the weights the brand font actually has; a missing weight renders as fake
bold.

**The end card.** The mark lands in the last 5–10 percent of the piece,
centred, the lockup about 15–30 percent of the frame width, and it is
performed rather than faded in: carried in, filled from below, or wiped in
beside the wordmark. Then the call to action, held as long as the app promo
close requires.

**Tells:** typing into a prompt box as the opener; a feature list as three
cards; a title over a dimmed screenshot; an invented dashboard; the logo docked
in a corner.

## Vertical social clips (9:16)

Built at 1080×1920 when the request or the platform says vertical. A landscape
piece is recomposed, never squeezed: rebuild side-by-side panels and wide
charts as a vertical sequence.

**The platform draws over the frame.** The top 12, bottom 20 and right 10
percent zones in type craft are the minimum. Feeds that show a long
description, a handle and buttons cover more: there, keep every word and the
product above roughly the bottom 30 percent, put only texture or decoration
below it, and keep text off the right-hand button rail. Ad placements reserve
more again; check the destination with `list_platform_outputs`.

**The first second.** Frame 0 is the thumbnail and the hook: a face, the
result, or one bold line already on screen; never black, never a logo. The
viewer decides in about a second, so the change or the question arrives in the
first beat. Many watch muted: the picture carries the hook without sound and
speech is captioned.

**Layout.** One subject, centred, stacked vertically. Type runs larger than in
landscape: about 1.3 times, titles 1.35, statistics 1.4, with panel padding
falling from 40–64 px to 24–36 px. Nothing meant to be read sits below the
type floor, and each line has a word limit (about five English words for a
claim, fewer for long Turkish words): cut words, never shrink the type. On
talking heads: push in on emphasis, cut B-roll on the sentence, and never put a
card over the face.

**Pacing.** Faster than landscape: shots of 1–2 s, a new beat every 2–4 s, the
payoff before the viewer swipes. A loop that cuts from the last frame back to
the first is a decision, not a default.

**Tells:** a squeezed landscape layout; text under the platform controls; a
logo or black first frame; a hook that only works with sound.

## Music-driven pieces

The song is the structure. Rights, beat grid and markers come from the music
workflow's music-led edits reference; this is the shape.

- Sections are chapters (intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro). Cut density
  follows energy: slower shots in verses, one picture per strong beat in the
  chorus, a held image on a breakdown.
- The chorus returns to one home stage and grows each time it comes back; the
  ending rhymes with the opening. Chapter breaks land on section boundaries,
  with a transition that belongs to the piece's medium (a brush wipe, a paper
  tear, a match).
- Each lyric line gets its own picture showing what the line means, not the
  line itself. Lyrics on screen only when asked, set as type rather than
  subtitles, each line held at least a beat.
- Something happens in every shot, and every cut has a cause on screen.
- A song about something real gets real material (footage, photographs,
  places); an abstract track gets one world in a strong medium. A physical
  medium (ink and watercolour, cut paper, risograph, grain on footage) carries
  a music piece further than effects; the style recipes have several.

**Tells:** one treatment held for more than about 6 s; a spectrum or waveform
(useAudio bands) as the main picture rather than an accent; a cut on every
single beat; words about the music instead of the song's own world.

## Characters and 3D

- A character gives every transition a cause and the piece its jokes: it
  carries the result in, rejects its own first try. A mood change goes through
  a take (anticipation, squash, the new face, a settle); a face never snaps.
- Offset the parts: the head leads, the arms follow, the amounts differ. Never
  move both arms, both eyes or a crowd in unison on one curve. Contact sells
  it: feet on the ground, props in the hand, eyes on what they look at. An
  idle breath or blink on a character is life, not a tell.
- Each action is one read: lead the eye to where it happens, play it fast, and
  hold the reaction. A caption repeating what the action already shows is the
  classic failure.
- 3D earns its place as a hero object, a product or a simulation that
  explains. One hero object beats a crowd; one push through it is worth ten
  orbits. Cutaways and labelled real numbers turn a simulation into an
  explanation. The 3D Studio skill owns light, materials and rendering.

## Interface and app demos

Build the interface as a camera world at full size and film it the way a
close screen recording would: the camera sits on the part that changes, so
the copy reads on a phone. The web promo kit gives the browser frame, cursor
and click ripple; the camera kit frames each control with its neighbours.

- Open on a short wide shot of the whole screen (overview, under 1.5 s), then
  one step every 2.5–4 s: push to the control, the cursor glides and clicks,
  the result appears, the camera follows the result.
- Fit the control together with its context; fitting a lone button
  over-zooms it (camera_zoom_high). UI copy at least 32 px on screen.
- Step captions of three to six words in a band the interface never uses, or
  pinned beside the control. A caption over a panel title or a row is the
  most common failure (text_covered, content_overlap).
- A thing that moves from one panel into another (an event into its slot) is
  a Carry, not a fade out and a fade in.
- Keep the finished state alive (a slow push, a highlight pulse) and end
  within two seconds of it.

## Code explainers

- Show few lines at a time, large (monospace 34 px and up on 1080p), with
  the line that matters highlighted while the rest dims. Type it with a cursor
  only when typing is the point; otherwise reveal whole lines.
- Pair every code beat with its effect: the output, the diagram or the screen
  the line produces, next to it or after a camera move, never only the code.
- A diff reads as red lines leaving and green lines arriving in place; keep
  the surrounding lines still so the eye finds the change.
- Check glyph coverage for arrows and box-drawing characters in the chosen
  monospace face (missing_glyph).

## Year and stats recaps

- Open on the year in huge type, already moving. Then one stat per moment,
  about 2.5 s each and cut on the beat, the number counting up large (160–260
  px on 1080p) with its own small moving illustration.
- Vary the layout per stat (number centred with the picture around it, the
  number inside a full-frame illustration, a camera move along a route, a
  split). The same corner layout every time reads as animated slides.
- Count from the earlier value to the new one ("96 to 214") while the
  picture grows with it; never hold the old number first.
- Keep drifting background shapes and confetti off the numbers, and end on
  the year rolling over with the last line landing late.

## Logo stings

The craft is in [technique recipes](technique-recipes.md) (logo sting): find
the hit first, use the logo's own paths (drawPath, or morph simple shapes into
them with matching parts: morph_no_pairs), lock the mark on the hit, reveal
the wordmark clear of the moving mark, and keep the last second moving.

## Long narrated films in one world

For a narrated piece longer than about 30 s whose scenes should feel like one
continuous shot (a journey, a cycle, a tour):

- Put the world, the moving characters and the camera shots in one module on
  an absolute clock in seconds. Each placed segment only renders
  start + its own time, so a cut between segments is invisible and a fix in
  the module republishes every segment at once.
- Synthesize the narration line by line first and cut the segments on the
  measured line boundaries; then time on-screen facts to the words by name
  (useWords and at("word")), so a re-recorded line moves its graphics with it.
- Keep each shot of the camera its own move when a long hold meets a quick
  move, and read the plan before rendering anything: review_motion_graphic
  with camera_only lists every hold and move with its framing.
- Captions sit in the lower band, fact cards in the upper band with an
  opaque backing, and the chapter label below the top 8% of a portrait frame.

## Tells of a generated piece, by type

Run this list over the beat frames before presenting a draft, together with
the general tells in [story and review](story-and-review.md):

| Type | Tells |
| --- | --- |
| Explainer | bullets on a slide, icons in circles, the whole diagram at once, narration echoed on screen |
| Data | KPI card grid, dashboard, pie chart, every number counting at once, unsourced figures |
| Launch or promo | prompt-typing opener, three feature cards, title over a dimmed screenshot, invented UI, docked logo |
| Vertical | squeezed landscape layout, words under platform controls, black or logo first frame, sound-only hook |
| Music | one treatment over 6 s, waveform as the picture, a cut on every beat, lyrics as subtitles |
| Character | parts moving in unison, faces snapping between moods, captions repeating the action |
