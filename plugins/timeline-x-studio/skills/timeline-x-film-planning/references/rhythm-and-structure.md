# Rhythm, beat types, and structure by length

Read this when pacing decides the plan: a beat list or production shot plan,
a piece longer than about a minute, or a pacing check before production. It
complements [craft gates](craft-gates.md) and the screenplay reference; it adds
no tool and authorizes no project edit.

## Plan sheet

Fit the plan on one screen. For each beat give the time range, its beat type,
the visible content, what the subject does, the camera (size, lens, move), what
the audience should grasp, and the sound. Below it, list any on-screen names
and the three biggest risks with a mitigation for each. Describe shots so an
illustrator could sketch them; mood adjectives such as "cinematic" or "moody"
on their own give nobody anything to draw.

Default to variety. Neighbouring shots differ in camera move and in size, and
no designed transition is used on two consecutive cuts, although hard cuts may
run in a row. Fresh information reaches the viewer every 2–4 s in energetic
work and roughly every 5–6 s in standard work; contemplative work relies on
long, slowly revealing holds. Identity is the constant: a single visual
language plus one recurring signature (a move, a motif, a sound) that comes
back altered.

## Beat types

Label each beat with one type. The label fixes how fast it moves, how often it
cuts, and how loud it sits. Pieces order the types differently, but a type
keeps its tempo wherever it appears.

| Type | Job | Pace | Cuts | Sound | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rest | Shows the world before anything happens | Held | None | Quiet | 1–1.5 s |
| Gather | Lets detail accumulate inside one framing | Held, with small moves | Up to one a second | Rising | 3–6 s |
| Transform | The subject's shape or condition alters | Stepped on half beats | One to five a second | An accent on the change | 2–3 s |
| Transit | Moves from one place to the next | One unbroken camera move | Only that move's own steps | A pad or groove that swells | 2–4 s |
| Montage | Covers many places at once | Steps that accelerate on the music grid | Three to eight a second | A steady groove | 5–9 s |
| Turn | Danger, or every thread converging | Slow build, then a flip within 1–3 frames | From none up to two a second | A drop, then the strongest accent of the piece | 1–3 s |
| Pause | Solitude and stillness | Held | None | The quietest point | 1–1.5 s |
| Meeting | The subject arrives at its purpose | Held, unhurried movement | Up to one a second | Warm and swelling | 3–7 s |
| Time skip | Signals that time has passed | Flicker on twos, or shifting light over one framing | Steps | No fresh onsets | About 1 s |
| Close | Title, final image, or loop to the start | Held | None | Fading or sustained | 2–4 s |

- Reserve the strongest accent for the turn; the meeting is warm, not loud.
- A fast stretch is usually one camera move advanced in steps on twos rather
  than a pile of separate shots.
- Justify each cut in a few spoken words: the process advances; the subject
  stays put while its surroundings change; one shape becomes another; we enter
  or leave the subject; the same subject at a far larger or smaller scale; the
  subject's point of view; a cause meets its effect; a jump forward in time; an
  earlier image returns with a new meaning. Cut first wherever no reason exists.
- Cut on movement, so an action that starts in one shot completes in the next,
  or cut on a sound.
- Fix the timing of narration and music before cutting picture; a temporary
  read is enough. Then edit the images against that audio.
- Flag five or more consecutive shots of roughly equal length, and any act or
  section whose pace never shifts.

## Tempo map, voice, and picture

- Mark every section as fast (cuts of 2–4 s), breathing (space for a spoken
  line to settle), or held (one long, still view). Avoid three sections in a row
  with the same mark; alternation creates the rhythm.
- Let narration and image alternate as the carrier. While the speaker is
  silent, the image supplies an event, a reveal, or a cut to something new;
  while the image stays still, a line or a sound supplies the movement. If both
  go quiet together, the stretch dies unless it is a deliberate hold or one of
  the key moments.
- With no narrator, move the information a voice would give onto the screen at
  a steady rate: type, figures, maps, labels, diagrams.
- Start a spoken line roughly 0.3–0.6 s after a cut, and let its key word fall
  within half a second of the image it refers to. In a narrated piece longer
  than a minute, the voice is heard by the two-second mark.

## Structure by length

For a story, first state five things in a line each: the situation, what the
protagonist wants, what stands in the way, the change, and the outcome. Then
say what carries the piece: one of the shapes below adapted to the idea, or a
form of its own such as a mood arc or a single image transforming. The idea
chooses the form; these shapes only help clarity and attention.

| Length | Shape | Curiosity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15–60 s | A single turn. Poetic, abstract, or looping work may follow kishōtenketsu (introduce, develop, twist, reconcile), which does without conflict | One question, raised at once, settled at the end |
| 30–60 s that explains or narrates | The same single turn, placed last and approached through linked questions | Every answer raises the following question; a new link roughly each 15–20 s |
| 1–5 min | A circular journey in proportion: comfort 0–10 %, want 10–20 %, crossing into the unfamiliar 20–30 %, trials 30–50 %, finding it 50–60 %, paying for it 60–75 %, return 75–90 %, changed 90–100 % | One large question posed in the first 15 s and resolved in the final fifth |
| 5 min and up | Break the expected pattern inside 5 s, establish why it matters with one hard fact before 30 s, get the story moving by then, and make each later section resolve part of the question while posing a narrower one | The promise below, kept near the end; something fresh every 10–15 s |

Linked questions belong only to the explaining 30–60 s row:

- If something can be lost, show it as an image inside the first 3 s.
- Withhold rather than ask: the framing hides part of it, a sketch halts
  midway, a counter stops. A question spoken to the viewer is the weaker form.
- Build small reversals toward the main one. Each link resolves the previous
  question and twists it without contradicting what the viewer saw; the single
  main turn still arrives last.
- Pass each question along through image, edit, and sound as well as words.

A story or cinematic piece finishes on a picture, not on an instruction to the
viewer. A channel video finishes at full energy rather than on a sign-off line.

## Long pieces: the promise

Beyond about five minutes, people keep watching because they want an answer.

- Early, within the first 30–60 s, set up what the ending will resolve: open on
  a striking moment taken from later, establish an expectation that will be
  broken, or state a contradiction the piece will settle. Say early who has
  something at stake and what it is, as a figure plus an image the viewer can
  keep in mind, such as a distance on a map or a deadline in hours.
- Track curiosity in a table: each question, the second it is raised, and the
  second it is resolved. Keep no more than two large questions open together,
  bring a reveal or reversal every 60–90 s, and resolve nothing before the
  viewer wants it resolved.
- Seed instead of explaining. Facts needed later enter as a question or one
  image, never as an early block that answers them in advance.
- The material sets the length. Extra minutes must bring extra events, not
  stretch the existing ones; if the material ends at seven minutes, so does the
  piece.
- Section cards are optional and depend on the genre's habits. When used, a card
  runs 2–3 s, shows a numeral and a brief heading in the display face, has its
  own tint, animates in and out, and carries a sound accent; never use a silent
  black frame. Each card rephrases the open question or names what the coming
  section resolves. Up to about three minutes, sections stay invisible. For a
  YouTube delivery, put the same sections with timestamps in the description.

## Pacing check before production

Before paid video production, review the pacing on a timed rough version that
uses the planned cut points, the final on-screen copy, and narration. The
lightest version presents the storyboard page's timed panels together with
the measured line lengths of a temporary narration. That narration is either
the user's own recording or a read made within the approved voiceover scope.

Playing it as a video changes the project, so build that only when the user
asks for or accepts it, on a separate Timeline from `create_sequence`, never on
the Timeline that Scene Manager fills. Place the approved storyboard stills or
plain colour cards at each beat's exact range, the final copy with
`add_text_layer`, and the temporary narration. If the connection cannot target
that Timeline, stop and say so rather than building it in the main edit.

In both cases put a single question to the user: does the pacing work, and
which shots feel too long or too short? Feed the answers into the plan before
production starts.
